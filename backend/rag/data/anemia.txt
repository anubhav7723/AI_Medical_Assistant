ANEMIA - COMPREHENSIVE MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE BASE

=======================================================
SECTION 1: DEFINITION & OVERVIEW
=======================================================

Anemia is a condition in which the number of red blood cells (RBCs) or the hemoglobin concentration within them is lower than normal, resulting in reduced oxygen transport capacity to the body's tissues and organs.

WHO Definition:
Anemia is defined by hemoglobin concentration thresholds that vary by age, sex, physiological status, and altitude.

WHO Global Burden:
- Anemia affects approximately 1.62 billion people worldwide (WHO Global Database on Anaemia)
- Accounts for 8.8% of the total global burden of disease
- Most prevalent in South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa
- Iron deficiency is the most common cause, responsible for approximately 50% of all anemia cases

Types of Anemia:
1. Iron Deficiency Anemia (IDA) — most common globally
2. Vitamin B12 Deficiency Anemia (Megaloblastic)
3. Folate Deficiency Anemia
4. Hemolytic Anemia (RBCs destroyed faster than produced)
5. Aplastic Anemia (bone marrow fails to produce enough cells)
6. Sickle Cell Anemia (genetic, abnormal hemoglobin shape)
7. Anemia of Chronic Disease (ACD) — associated with inflammation
8. Thalassemia (genetic disorder affecting hemoglobin production)

SECTION 2: WHO DIAGNOSTIC THRESHOLDS & NORMAL RANGES
=======================================================

WHO Hemoglobin Cutoffs for Anemia Diagnosis:

| Population Group                  | Non-Anemic (g/dL) | Mild Anemia | Moderate Anemia | Severe Anemia |
|-----------------------------------|-------------------|-------------|-----------------|---------------|
| Children 6–59 months              | ≥11.0             | 10.0–10.9   | 7.0–9.9         | <7.0          |
| Children 5–11 years               | ≥11.5             | 11.0–11.4   | 8.0–10.9        | <8.0          |
| Children 12–14 years              | ≥12.0             | 11.0–11.9   | 8.0–10.9        | <8.0          |
| Non-pregnant women (≥15 years)    | ≥12.0             | 11.0–11.9   | 8.0–10.9        | <8.0          |
| Pregnant women                    | ≥11.0             | 10.0–10.9   | 7.0–9.9         | <7.0          |
| Men (≥15 years)                   | ≥13.0             | 11.0–12.9   | 8.0–10.9        | <8.0          |

Source: WHO. Haemoglobin concentrations for the diagnosis of anaemia and assessment of severity. Geneva, 2011.

Clinical Normal Ranges (Complete Blood Count):

Hemoglobin:
- Men: 13.5–17.5 g/dL
- Women: 12.0–15.5 g/dL
- Pregnant women: ≥11.0 g/dL (WHO)

Red Blood Cell Count (RBC):
- Men: 4.5–5.9 million cells/mcL
- Women: 4.1–5.1 million cells/mcL

Hematocrit (Packed Cell Volume - PCV):
- Men: 40–52%
- Women: 36–48%

Mean Corpuscular Volume (MCV):
- Normal: 80–100 fL
- <80 fL = Microcytic (suggests iron deficiency or thalassemia)
- >100 fL = Macrocytic (suggests B12/folate deficiency)

Mean Corpuscular Hemoglobin (MCH):
- Normal: 27–33 pg
- Low MCH = Hypochromic anemia

Mean Corpuscular Hemoglobin Concentration (MCHC):
- Normal: 32–36 g/dL
- Low MCHC = Hypochromic

Red Cell Distribution Width (RDW):
- Normal: 11.5–14.5%
- High RDW indicates anisocytosis (variation in RBC size)

Serum Ferritin (Iron Stores):
- Men: 30–300 ng/mL
- Women: 15–200 ng/mL
- <15 ng/mL = Iron deficiency confirmed (WHO)
- <30 ng/mL = Iron depletion likely

Serum Iron:
- Normal: 60–170 mcg/dL

Total Iron Binding Capacity (TIBC):
- Normal: 250–370 mcg/dL
- High TIBC with low iron = Iron deficiency

Transferrin Saturation:
- Normal: 20–50%
- <16% = Iron deficiency anemia

Reticulocyte Count:
- Normal: 0.5–1.5% of RBCs
- High = Active RBC production (good response to treatment or blood loss)
- Low in aplastic anemia

Additional CBC Parameters:
WBC (White Blood Cells): 4,500–11,000 cells/mcL
Platelets: 150,000–400,000 cells/mcL
Neutrophils: 55–70%
Lymphocytes: 20–40%
PDW (Platelet Distribution Width): 10–17 fL
PCT (Plateletcrit): 0.1–0.5%

SECTION 3: SYMPTOMS & CLINICAL PRESENTATION
=======================================================

Early/Mild Anemia:
- Fatigue and decreased energy
- Mild pallor (pale skin, conjunctiva)
- Slight reduction in exercise tolerance

Moderate Anemia:
- Noticeable fatigue and weakness
- Shortness of breath on exertion
- Palpitations and faster heart rate
- Headaches and difficulty concentrating
- Cold hands and feet
- Brittle nails

Severe Anemia:
- Extreme fatigue, inability to perform daily tasks
- Chest pain and angina
- Rapid or irregular heartbeat
- Dizziness and fainting
- Swollen legs and ankles (fluid retention)
- Pale or yellowish skin
- Confusion and cognitive impairment

Condition-Specific Signs:
- Iron Deficiency: Pica (craving ice, clay), koilonychia (spoon-shaped nails), angular cheilitis
- B12 Deficiency: Tingling/numbness in hands and feet, cognitive changes, glossitis (smooth tongue)
- Hemolytic Anemia: Jaundice, dark urine, splenomegaly
- Thalassemia: Bone deformities, growth retardation in children

=======================================================
SECTION 4: CAUSES & RISK FACTORS
=======================================================

Causes by Mechanism:

A. Decreased RBC Production:
- Iron deficiency (poor diet, malabsorption, chronic bleeding)
- Vitamin B12 or folate deficiency
- Bone marrow failure (aplastic anemia, leukemia)
- Chronic kidney disease (reduced erythropoietin)
- Hypothyroidism, lead poisoning

B. Increased RBC Destruction (Hemolysis):
- Sickle cell disease
- Thalassemia
- Autoimmune hemolytic anemia
- G6PD deficiency
- Malaria (major cause in endemic regions)

C. Blood Loss:
- Acute: trauma, surgery, internal bleeding
- Chronic: GI bleeding (ulcers, colorectal cancer), heavy menstruation (menorrhagia)

Risk Factors (WHO Highlights):
- Pregnant women: highest risk due to expanded blood volume and fetal demands
- Children under 5: rapid growth increases iron demands
- Adolescent girls: menstrual losses
- Elderly: chronic disease and poor nutrition
- Vegetarians/vegans: lower heme-iron intake
- People in malaria-endemic regions

=======================================================
SECTION 5: ML FEATURE INSIGHTS & DIAGNOSTIC PATTERNS
=======================================================

Primary Predictive Features:
- Hemoglobin: Single strongest indicator. Values below WHO cutoffs classify anemia type by severity.
- MCV: Differentiates microcytic (<80 fL), normocytic (80–100 fL), macrocytic (>100 fL) anemia
- MCH & MCHC: Identifies hypochromic anemia (low color = low iron content per cell)
- RDW: High RDW + low MCV = Iron deficiency anemia (mixed population of small cells)
- Serum Ferritin: Most sensitive marker for iron stores depletion
- Reticulocyte Count: Measures marrow response — important for treatment monitoring

Diagnostic Algorithm Patterns:
1. Low Hgb + Low MCV + Low Ferritin → Iron Deficiency Anemia
2. Low Hgb + High MCV + Low B12 → Megaloblastic Anemia
3. Low Hgb + Normal MCV + High RDW → Mixed deficiency or early anemia
4. Low Hgb + Elevated Bilirubin + Reticulocytosis → Hemolytic Anemia
5. Low Hgb + Low Reticulocyte → Aplastic/Bone marrow failure

Severity Classification for ML:
- Mild: Hgb 10–12 g/dL (women), 11–13 g/dL (men)
- Moderate: Hgb 7–10 g/dL
- Severe: Hgb <7 g/dL → medical emergency

=======================================================
SECTION 6: TREATMENT & MANAGEMENT (WHO GUIDELINES)
=======================================================

Iron Deficiency Anemia:
- WHO recommends daily oral iron supplementation
  - Adults: 120 mg elemental iron/day
  - Pregnant women: 30–60 mg elemental iron + 400 mcg folic acid daily (WHO 2016 ANC guidelines)
  - Children 6–23 months in high-prevalence areas: 10–12.5 mg/day elemental iron
- Treatment duration: 3–6 months to replenish stores after Hgb normalizes
- IV iron for those who cannot tolerate oral or have severe malabsorption

Vitamin B12 Deficiency:
- Intramuscular B12 injections (cyanocobalamin or hydroxocobalamin)
- Oral high-dose B12 (1000–2000 mcg/day) if malabsorption is not the cause

Folate Deficiency:
- Folic acid 1–5 mg/day orally for 4 months

Severe/Refractory Anemia:
- Blood transfusion when Hgb <7 g/dL or symptomatic
- Erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs) for anemia of chronic kidney disease
- Bone marrow transplant for aplastic anemia or thalassemia major

WHO Preventive Strategies:
1. Food fortification programs (iron-fortified flour, cereals)
2. Iron and folic acid supplementation in pregnancy
3. Malaria prevention and treatment in endemic areas
4. Deworming programs (helminths cause blood loss)
5. Dietary diversification campaigns

SECTION 7: LIFESTYLE, DIET & NUTRITION
=======================================================

Iron-Rich Foods (Heme Iron — best absorbed, from animal sources):
- Red meat (beef, lamb)
- Liver and organ meats
- Poultry (chicken, turkey)
- Seafood (oysters, sardines, tuna)

Iron-Rich Foods (Non-Heme Iron — from plant sources, less absorbed):
- Spinach, kale, broccoli
- Lentils, chickpeas, kidney beans
- Tofu and tempeh
- Pumpkin seeds, quinoa, fortified cereals

Absorption Enhancers:
- Vitamin C (citrus, tomatoes, bell peppers) — increases non-heme iron absorption by up to 3x
- Eat with meat or fish (meat factor enhances plant iron absorption)

Absorption Inhibitors (avoid with iron-rich meals):
- Tea, coffee (tannins)
- Calcium supplements or dairy
- Phytates (whole grains eaten in excess)
- Antacids

Foods Rich in B12:
- Eggs, dairy products, meat, fish
- Fortified plant milks (for vegans)

Foods Rich in Folate:
- Dark leafy greens, avocado, legumes, fortified grains

=======================================================
SECTION 8: COMPLICATIONS & PROGNOSIS
=======================================================

Untreated Anemia Complications:
- Cardiac: Compensatory tachycardia → cardiac hypertrophy → heart failure
- Cognitive: Impaired concentration, memory, and school performance in children
- Pregnancy: Preterm birth, low birth weight, maternal mortality (WHO)
- Immune: Increased susceptibility to infections
- Growth: Stunted physical and mental development in children

WHO Mortality Data:
- Severe anemia contributes to 40% of maternal deaths in developing countries
- In children, severe anemia is a major contributor to malaria-related mortality

=======================================================
SECTION 9: MONITORING & FOLLOW-UP
=======================================================

Recommended Monitoring Schedule:
- Initial diagnosis: Full CBC + serum ferritin + peripheral smear
- After 2–4 weeks of iron therapy: Reticulocyte count (response check)
- After 4–8 weeks: Repeat Hgb (expect 1–2 g/dL rise)
- After Hgb normalizes: Continue iron for 3–6 more months
- Long-term: Annual CBC for at-risk populations (pregnant women, elderly, CKD patients)

SECTION 10: Q&A FOR CHATBOT (EXTENDED)
=======================================================

Q: Why is my hemoglobin low?
A: Low hemoglobin means your blood carries less oxygen, causing fatigue and weakness. Common causes include iron deficiency (most common globally per WHO), vitamin B12 or folate deficiency, blood loss from menstruation or GI bleeding, or chronic diseases like kidney failure. A full CBC with ferritin test helps identify the exact cause.

Q: What should I eat to improve anemia?
A: Eat iron-rich foods like red meat, liver, lentils, spinach, and fortified cereals. Pair plant-based iron with vitamin C sources (citrus, tomatoes) to enhance absorption. Avoid tea, coffee, or calcium within an hour of iron-rich meals as they block absorption. For B12 deficiency, include eggs, dairy, and fish.

Q: Is anemia dangerous?
A: Mild anemia is manageable but moderate to severe anemia can impair heart function, cognitive performance, and immune response. WHO identifies severe anemia (Hgb <7 g/dL) as a medical emergency. In pregnancy, untreated anemia is a leading cause of maternal death in low-income countries.

Q: What is the difference between microcytic and macrocytic anemia?
A: Microcytic anemia (MCV <80 fL) features small RBCs, usually caused by iron deficiency or thalassemia. Macrocytic anemia (MCV >100 fL) features large RBCs, caused by vitamin B12 or folate deficiency. Normal-sized RBCs with low hemoglobin (normocytic) often indicate anemia of chronic disease.

Q: How do I know if my ferritin level is low?
A: WHO defines iron deficiency as serum ferritin below 15 ng/mL. Levels between 15–30 ng/mL indicate depleted stores. A low ferritin combined with low hemoglobin and low MCV strongly confirms iron deficiency anemia.

Q: What does high RDW mean in my blood test?
A: RDW (Red Cell Distribution Width) measures how much your RBC sizes vary. A high RDW (>14.5%) means your red cells are unequal in size (anisocytosis), which is a hallmark of iron deficiency anemia. It can also be elevated in mixed nutritional deficiencies.

Q: Can anemia cause heart problems?
A: Yes. Chronic severe anemia forces the heart to pump harder to compensate for poor oxygen delivery, which can lead to an enlarged heart (cardiomegaly) and eventually heart failure. The risk is higher in elderly patients and those with pre-existing cardiovascular disease.

Q: Do pregnant women need extra iron?
A: Yes. WHO recommends 30–60 mg of elemental iron daily throughout pregnancy, combined with 400 mcg folic acid. The demand for iron nearly doubles during pregnancy to support fetal development and expanded blood volume.

Q: What is thalassemia and how is it different from iron deficiency anemia?
A: Thalassemia is a genetic disorder where hemoglobin production is reduced due to abnormal genes. Like iron deficiency, it shows low MCV and MCH, but ferritin levels are normal or elevated, and blood smear shows target cells. It does not improve with iron supplementation.

Q: When should I go to the doctor for anemia?
A: Seek medical attention if you have severe fatigue, chest pain, rapid heartbeat, fainting, or pallor. Hgb below 8 g/dL requires urgent evaluation. Pregnant women, children, and elderly individuals should be screened routinely even without symptoms.
